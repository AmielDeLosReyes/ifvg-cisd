## Concepts

### Judas Swing (False Breakout / Swing Trap)
- **Kind:** definition · price pattern
- **What ICT says:** A sharp, directional move at the open (often on high volume or a gap) that goes one way (e.g., up), reaches a short-term high, then reverses sharply in the opposite direction. Named after Judas's betrayal—the move "tricks" traders into thinking the direction is set when it's actually engineered to trap them. "Right off of a Judas swing which is one directional rate at the 9:30 opening regular trading hours."
- **How to identify:** Look for a directional move at the open (9:30 ET for NQ) that creates a short-term high/low without consolidation; follows that with a sharp reversal (typically a candle or two) that breaks back down.
- **How to use:** When you see a Judas swing, expect a reversal shortly after. Use it as a sell signal if it's an upward Judas (short the breakout). Anticipate that stops above the short-term high will be run before the main down move; place your stop just above the short-term high or use a wider stop to account for this.
- **Example in this video:** NQ opens at 9:30 with a gap up (premium gap from Friday's close). Price runs straight up in one shot to a short-term high without consolidation. This is the Judas swing. Reverses sharply on the very next candle. ICT shorted this move.

### Engineered Liquidity & Buy-Side / Sell-Side Distribution
- **Kind:** definition · market structure
- **What ICT says:** Market makers create the appearance of strength (or weakness) by distributing (or accumulating) positions at key levels. On a Judas swing (false breakout up), market makers will allow price to rip higher to reach old resistance levels, sell heavily into the move to retail traders who see "confirmation," then dump it lower. This is called "engineering buy-side liquidity"—creating the appearance of buying pressure to trap buyers. "Smart money can go long here, sell, and sell more to the engineered liquidity that it forms." Bodies stay out of key levels; wicks may touch, but bodies won't, signifying that the move is engineered redistribution, not organic buying.
- **How to identify:** (1) Price reaches an old high or resistance level (relative equal high, previous swing high, etc.). (2) Bodies cannot touch or cannot stay above a key level (like consequent encroachment), but wicks do. (3) Price fails to go higher despite reaching an old level. (4) The failure is followed by a sharp reversal.
- **How to use:** When you see engineered liquidity signatures (bodies avoiding a level, wicks touching but bodies above), expect a reversal. Sell into the engineered move (short at the engineered buy-side; long at engineered sell-side). This is a higher-probability trade because you're front-running the reversal.
- **Example in this video:** 9:30 opening: price runs up in Judas swing, wicks reach up to touch upper levels (consequent encroachment between the hourly low and high, octant levels), but bodies cannot touch them. This signals engineered buy-side distribution. ICT shorted this.

### Octant / Quadrant (Half-Level / 25/75 Splits)
- **Kind:** definition · price level
- **What ICT says:** When measuring a range, divide it into four equal parts (quarters). The octant or quadrant levels are the 25% and 75% lines within that range. For a range with a low of X and high of Y, the octants are X + (Y−X)×0.25 and X + (Y−X)×0.75. "This is an octant... the uh 375 level." Used to identify price levels where order flow is likely to stall. (Note: "octant" here seems to refer to quarterly divisions, not the traditional 8-part octant.)
- **How to identify:** Mark a key range (e.g., the low and high of a swing, or the gap range). Divide that into quarters. The lines at 25% and 75% are octant levels.
- **How to use:** Use as secondary support/resistance. Bodies avoiding octant levels indicate engineered moves; bodies respecting octants indicate organic moves.
- **Example in this video:** The range between the hourly low and the 1H buy-side balance sell-side efficiency high has octant levels. Price fails to touch or sustain above these octants around 0:14:45, 0:22:43, 0:28:30.

### Breaker Block (Shift of Market Structure)
- **Kind:** definition · price pattern · market structure
- **What ICT says:** A three-candle pattern: higher high, lower low, then higher high again. Once this pattern completes, the previous "lower low" is no longer a support; it's been "broken." The pattern signals a shift in market structure and confirms that buyers have taken control and are now in command. "High, low, higher high. This is your breaker."
- **How to identify:** Look for three candlesticks: (1) higher high, (2) lower low, (3) higher high again. When confirmed, the pattern shifts bias to bullish.
- **How to use:** Once a breaker is formed, do not place shorts below the lower low. If price does pull back below the lower low after a breaker, it's a "failure" and signals a reversal back down (not a breakout continuation). Use the breaker as a bias confirmation—if it forms without bodies going below it, it's a bullish breaker.
- **Example in this video:** Marked around 0:20:22. Breaker forms on intraday chart; price goes higher high, then down to lower low, then higher high again. No bodies trade below the lower low of the breaker, confirming its validity.
- **Timestamps:**

### Event Horizon / Midpoint Between PD Arrays
- **Kind:** definition · price level
- **What ICT says:** The midpoint between two key price delivery arrays (FVGs, order blocks, highs, lows, etc.). This is a zone where price often stalls or reverses because it's the geometric center of a range. "Event horizon. It's a midpoint between two key PD arrays."
- **How to identify:** Identify two key levels (e.g., a swing high and swing low, or two gaps). Calculate the midpoint between them. That's the event horizon.
- **How to use:** Use as a secondary target or stall zone. Expect bodies to struggle to cross through event horizons without a reversal or consolidation.
- **Example in this video:** Event horizon mentioned around 0:14:45 between the hourly efficiency and the opening range gap.
- **Timestamps:**

### Short-Term High/Low
- **Kind:** definition · price level
- **What ICT says:** The highest or lowest price reached within the most recent few candles (e.g., the last 5 candles on the 1-minute chart). Used to identify potential reversal zones or confirmation of trend changes. "We create a short-term high and give me evidence around consequent encouragement."
- **How to identify:** On the 1m or 5m chart, mark the high/low of the last 3–5 candles.
- **How to use:** Use as a confirmation level. If price is reverting below a short-term high and showing bodies rejecting above it, that's bearish. Use as a stop-placement guide.
- **Example in this video:** The 9:30 opening Judas swing creates a short-term high; later price reverses below it.

### Full Gap Closure
- **Kind:** definition · price target
- **What ICT says:** When price has opened with a gap (e.g., gap up from previous close), a "full gap closure" occurs when price retraces all the way back down to fill that entire gap, reaching the previous session's close or the low of the gap range. Distinguished from "half-gap" (midpoint of gap) or "consequent encroachment of the gap" (midpoint of gap).
- **How to identify:** Mark the opening gap (high and low of the gap range between sessions). A full closure means price reaches the low of that gap.
- **How to use:** Use as a longer-term target when in a downtrend. If you shorted a gap-up move, a full gap closure is a viable final target.
- **Example in this video:** Mentioned as a potential target around 0:27:29 if price continues lower.

## Rules, Checklists & Numbers Stated

- Opening range gap (9:30 ET for NQ) is a powerful reference; use the gap low/high and midpoint (consequent encroachment of gap) as primary targets.
- When price runs in one direction (Judas swing) at the open without consolidation, expect reversal within the next 1–3 candles.
- Bodies tell the story; wicks are noise. If bodies avoid a key level (consequent encroachment, octants, relative equal highs), the move is engineered and will reverse.
- Place stops just above the short-term high (for shorts) or just below the short-term low (for longs) to allow one more spike but to exit before a full reversal.
- Use multiple timeframes (daily, hourly, 1-minute) to confirm entries. Daily bias + hourly structure + 1-minute execution.
- Distribute exits across multiple levels (relative equal lows, order blocks, final target at gap midpoint) rather than trying to catch the whole move.
- Do NOT share exact stop losses publicly if you have a large following—it will be run.
- Use event horizons (midpoints between key levels) as secondary stall/reversal zones.
- Breaker blocks confirm shift in structure; once formed, don't short below them (expect reversal).

## Glossary Candidates

`Judas Swing` — A directional move at the open (e.g., up) that reaches a short-term high without consolidation, then reverses sharply. Named for its "betrayal" of traders who chase the initial direction.

`Engineered Liquidity` — When market makers create the appearance of buying or selling pressure to trap retail traders, then reverse sharply. Identified by bodies avoiding key levels while wicks touch them.

`Breaker Block` — A three-candle pattern (higher high, lower low, higher high) that signals a shift in market structure. Once formed, the lower low is no longer support; price reverting below it is a failure.

`Octant / Quadrant` — One of four equal divisions of a price range; the 25% and 75% price levels within a range. Used to identify order flow and engineered moves.

`Event Horizon` — The midpoint between two key price delivery arrays; a zone where price often stalls or reverses due to geometric significance.

`Short-Term High/Low` — The highest or lowest price reached in the last few candles (typically 3–5 candles on 1m or 5m chart); used to confirm trend and place stops.

`Full Gap Closure` — Price retracing all the way back to fill an opening gap, reaching the previous session's close or the low of the gap range.

`Stop Loss Running` — The market deliberately targeting and hitting a trader's stop loss, especially if the stop is publicly known or at a predictable level (e.g., above a short-term high).
