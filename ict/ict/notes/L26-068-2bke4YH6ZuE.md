## Summary
Michael reviews a low-probability post-holiday Monday, explaining why market conditions were unfavorable for trading despite technically clean setups. He teaches that US bank holidays occurring on weekends create Monday low-participation days (best avoided), discusses the "lay of the land" scanning process, and emphasizes risk management, patience, and avoiding FOMO-driven trades. Key lesson: know when probabilities are against you and dial back participation rather than forcing trades into low-conviction environments. Also teaches: use if-then statements, not predictive absolutes, when analyzing market structure.

## Concepts

### US Holiday Effect on Market Participation (aliases: low participation days, holiday Mondays, no-touch days)
- **Kind:** rule + risk management
- **What ICT says:** When a US bank holiday falls on Saturday or Sunday, the following Monday (the actual observed holiday) is a "no-touch" day. Money stays on the sidelines; participation is predictably low. Low participation = wider bid-ask, less sustained price runs, more chop, and lower-probability setups. If you're starting the week with no established bias (price in middle of range), conviction is hard to come by. Never participate in these days; tape-read, demo-trade, or step away entirely. Missing a move on a low-probability day is better than taking a loss.
- **How to identify:** Check the US holiday calendar; if a major bank holiday (one that would close banks on weekdays) occurs on Saturday or Sunday, mark Monday as a no-trade day.
- **How to use:** Skip live trading on these days. If you feel the urge to trade, step away from the charts. Go do something physical. The discipline of NOT trading is as important as the discipline of trading well. Demo trading or tape reading is OK to learn structure, but not real money.
- **Example in this video:** July 7, 2026 (day after July 4 holiday, which fell on Saturday). Michael warned on X ahead of time not to trade. He tested it anyway to show how probabilities are stacked against you on these days, and the precision was off—he got stopped out on a trade.

- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** This is a probability rule, not a hard rule (edges can happen anytime); but compliance saves capital and reduces drawdown variance.

### Lay of the Land (aliases: LOL, scanning, reading the range)
- **Kind:** process / scanning method
- **What ICT says:** Before the market opens, scan the daily/15m chart and identify: the high and low of the range you're expecting, the key inefficiencies (major buy-side and sell-side liquidity), and minor imbalances. This "lay of the land" is your thesis—what price levels will be in play today. Draw the dealing range high/low, then mark the extreme inefficiencies within that range (one at the low, one at the high). Mark minor buy-side and minor sell-side within that range. This is your map. Don't overcomplicate it; if there are more than 4–5 zones, simplify.
- **How to identify:** Open your daily or 15m chart; identify the recent swing high and low; mark inefficiencies (gaps, FVGs, order blocks) within that range.
- **How to use:** Use LOL as your bias for the day. If price opens at the midpoint (equilibrium), you have no edge; wait for price to show directional intent before committing capital. If price opens near the high, expect a draw to the low (and watch the minor zones on the way). LOL also sets your targets and stop zones.
- **Example in this video:** July 7 LOL was a 15m range from high to low, with two extreme inefficiencies (one at each end of range) and two minor zones inside. Michael used this map to know where to expect bounces and reversals.
- **Timestamps:** [0:00:00](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=0) (LOL: 15m inefficiencies shown) · [0:03:18](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=198) (LOL on 15m and scaling to 1m) · [0:14:36](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=876) (LOL: range high, range low, 50% Fibonacci midpoint; opened at equilibrium = no edge) · [0:15:23](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=923) (LOL: dealing range high/low, minor buy-side, minor sell-side).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** If you open at 50% midpoint with no directional bias, admit there's no edge and wait for price to move 1–2% away from center before committing.

### Quadrant and Octant Levels (aliases: price quadrants, octants, 25% and 12.5% levels)
- **Kind:** measurement tool
- **What ICT says:** Divide a range into quadrants (25% increments) and octants (12.5% increments) using Fibonacci levels or price divisions. These levels are where smart money expects reversals and pauses. When price reaches a lower quadrant but fails to reach the octant above it, that's a directional signal. Michael marks quadrants and octants on the daily/15m chart to know where expected pauses or rejection zones are.
- **How to identify:** Draw Fibonacci or divide the high-to-low range into 4 (quadrants) or 8 (octants); mark each level.
- **How to use:** Quadrant/octant levels are interim targets. If price is moving down and fails to reach a lower octant from a higher quadrant, it's weak; expect reversal. Use them as intermediate stops or partial-profit zones.
- **Example in this video:** On the 15m chart, Michael marked quadrants; price traded to the lower quadrant but failed to reach the octant above it, signaling bearish bias (no commitment to higher octants).
- **Timestamps:** [0:00:49](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=49) (lower quadrant reached; failed to reach octant above).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Quadrants/octants are mathematical subdivisions; they're not price-action-specific but serve as algorithmic reference points.

### Volume Imbalance and First Presented Fair Value Gap (aliases: FPFVG, first imbalance gap, open-close gap)
- **Kind:** definition
- **What ICT says:** Volume imbalance is when a candle's open-to-close gap is significant (more than a tick or two). This creates an inefficiency—a fair value gap. The first presented FVG is the first gap that forms when price enters a new move. Mark the high of the lower candle and the low of the next candle (if gap exists); that's your FVG. On subsequent retracements, this same FVG may act as support or resistance.
- **How to identify:** Scan for gaps between candle closes and next candle opens; mark the open-close difference as an imbalance.
- **How to use:** FVGs are targets for reversals and bounces. Track the first presented FVG from a move; on subsequent pulls, price often tests it or uses it as a reversal zone.
- **Example in this video:** First potential FVG formed between two 1m candlesticks at the open; this was the volume imbalance between their close and next open. Later in the session, price retested this FVG and acted as an inversion FVG (weak retest = reversal).
- **Timestamps:** [0:08:55](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=535) (first potential FVG: volume imbalance between close and next open) · [0:10:34](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=634) (first position FVG retraced; bodies at consequent encroachment) · [0:11:23](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=683) (first presented FVG as target on pullback).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** First presented FVGs change role based on direction; track them for multiple sessions as they become targets on reversals.

### Draw on Liquidity (aliases: draw, liquidity draw, institutional target)
- **Kind:** rule
- **What ICT says:** When price action points toward a specific inefficiency or liquidity pool, smart money is "drawing" price toward it. This is the institutional algorithm doing its work. If price is moving toward a draw and showing no weakness, expect it to complete the draw. If it's weakening before reaching, the draw is questioned. A draw is characterized by sustained directional movement with clean structure (no choppy wicks or reversals).
- **How to identify:** Identify an inefficiency that price is moving toward; watch for commitment (bodies aligned, wicks controlled). If commitment is strong, you're watching a draw.
- **How to use:** Don't fight a draw; if price is drawing to a level, position trades in that direction. Use stops above (for shorts) or below (for longs) the structure showing the draw. Draws often complete cleanly.
- **Example in this video:** Michael was watching for a potential draw on liquidity lower in the evening/early Asian session after the Monday close. If price could "gravitate down towards that" and set up structure implying a continued draw, he'd engage.
- **Timestamps:** [0:08:08](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=488) (potential draw on liquidity lower) · [0:08:55](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=535) (using event horizon and draw logic).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Draws are directional; they're institutional moves, not retail chop. Recognize the difference between chop (rangebound wicks) and draws (committed bodies).

### If-Then Statement Approach (aliases: conditional analysis, probability framing)
- **Kind:** process / psychology
- **What ICT says:** Instead of making absolute predictions ("price will go down to X"), frame analysis as if-then statements ("If price takes out the buy-side here AND closes below CE, THEN I'm looking for a draw to the sell-side"). This removes ego and adaptation; you're not defending a prediction; you're watching to see if conditions are met. If conditions aren't met, you pivot to the next if-then without losing conviction. This is the opposite of confirmation bias.
- **How to identify:** Write your analysis as conditional statements: "If this happens, then I'm looking for this."
- **How to use:** Use if-then to stay flexible. If price does A, you expect B; if price does C instead, you're looking for D. Both are prepared responses; no surprises, no ego injury.
- **Example in this video:** Michael stated: "If we roll over and take out that low and accelerate and get below here on a sustained run, THEN I'd like to see it make an attempt to get down to this inefficiency." This is if-then thinking. If price doesn't take out the low, the condition isn't met; he has another if-then ready.
- **Timestamps:** [0:17:01](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=1021) (if then statement: "If it rolls through these lows, then I'd like to see it attempt here") · [0:17:48](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=1068) (if market proves it wants to do specific things, then I align my thinking).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** If-then removes the emotional burden of being "right"; you're just responding to market conditions.

### Dealing Range High and Low (aliases: daily range, range bounds)
- **Kind:** measurement / bias framework
- **What ICT says:** The high and low of the expected trading range for a day (or timeframe). Mark these clearly on your chart. Everything between them is fair game; nothing outside is part of today's range (until proven otherwise). Inefficiencies within the range are the targets; inefficiencies at the range edges are extremes.
- **How to identify:** Identify the swing high and swing low you expect for the day/week; mark them prominently.
- **How to use:** Use dealing range bounds as reference points for targets and stops. If price breaks outside the range, a new range is forming. If it stays within, use internal inefficiencies for trades.
- **Example in this video:** Michael marked dealing range high and low on the 15m chart; all subsequent moves were analyzed within that context. Minor inefficiencies within the range were targets; extremes at the edges were long-term goals.
- **Timestamps:** [0:16:13](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=973) (dealing range high and dealing range low, with minor buy and sell inside).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Ranges shift as price breaks bounds; be ready to update your dealing range if structure breaks.

### Minor Buy-Side and Minor Sell-Side Liquidity (aliases: minor buy-side, minor sell-side, internal liquidity pools)
- **Kind:** definition
- **What ICT says:** Within a dealing range, there are inefficiencies at the edges (extreme/major) and inefficiencies inside the range (minor). Minor buy-side liquidity is an imbalance where buyers are resting in the middle of a range (not at the top). Minor sell-side is the same but for sellers. These are "secondary" liquidity pools that may be swept en route to the major (extreme) pools.
- **How to identify:** Identify the dealing range high and low; scan inside for smaller gaps or imbalances; those are minor zones.
- **How to use:** Use minor liquidity pools as intermediate targets. If you're short and moving lower, expect price to touch the minor sell-side on the way down to the extreme sell-side. These are bounce points or partial-profit zones.
- **Example in this video:** Michael identified minor buy-side and minor sell-side within the 15m dealing range; price took out the minor buy-side in the morning, then failed to push higher and eventually worked toward the minor sell-side and extreme sell-side lower.
- **Timestamps:** [0:16:13](https://youtu.be/2bke4YH6ZuE?t=973) (minor buy side and minor sell side inside the dealing range).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Minor zones are secondary targets; don't mistake them for major support/resistance.

## Rules, checklists & numbers stated
- US bank holidays on weekends = Monday is a no-touch day; skip live trading.
- If opening at 50% range midpoint (equilibrium), there is no edge; wait for directional confirmation.
- Lay of the land: identify dealing range, extreme inefficiencies, and minor zones before 9:30 ET open.
- Quadrant/octant levels: if price reaches lower quadrant but fails octant above, it's bearish.
- Volume imbalance (open-close gap) = inefficiency; first presented FVG acts as target on retracements.
- Draws are committed moves; recognize bodies vs. chop (wicks).
- If-then statements: frame analysis as conditions, not predictions.
- First presented FVG: track across multiple sessions; it becomes a target on reversals.
- Partial profits at minor zones; runners/targets at extreme inefficiencies.
- Low-participation days: expect wider bid-ask, chop, lower precision.

## Process, risk & psychology
- **Avoid FOMO:** "Give yourself permission to miss things because you can't do anything about it once it happens." FOMO-driven entries on low-probability days are losses waiting to happen.
- **Summer doldrums:** July and August are traditionally low-volume months. Dial back participation. Look for clear alignment; if you don't see it, don't trade.
- **Conviction test:** "If you don't feel one-sided about your analysis, then it's not high probability and you're not holding a strong conviction." Use this as a filter: only trade when analysis is clear and one-directional.
- **Demo trading is OK:** If you feel the urge to participate but market conditions are weak, demo trade or tape read instead of risking real capital. This keeps you engaged without bleeding capital.
- **Regret management:** More traders get wrecked by trading low-probability days than by missing moves. Accepting missed moves is part of discipline.
- **Changing your analysis:** If price doesn't do what your initial analysis suggested, change your opinion or remove yourself. Don't force the trade. "Either change your opinion or remove yourself entirely"—this is the hardest decision for traders.
- **Simplify:** If a chart has more than a few zones, you're overcomplicating it. Simplify to dealing range, extreme inefficiencies, and minor zones only.

`Volume imbalance` — a gap between candle open and close; creates a fair value gap inefficiency.
`First presented FVG (FPFVG)` — the first gap that forms when price enters a new move; becomes a target on subsequent reversals.
`Minor buy-side / minor sell-side` — secondary liquidity pools within a dealing range (not at the edges); intermediate targets.
`Draw on liquidity` — an institutional algorithm move toward a specific inefficiency; characterized by committed bodies and sustained direction.

`US holiday no-touch day` — Monday after a weekend US bank holiday; low participation = avoid live trading.
`Equilibrium opening` — when price opens at the 50% midpoint of a range with no directional bias; no edge; wait for confirmation.
