## Concepts

### Buy-Side Imbalance / Sell-Side Efficiency (aliases: inefficiency, BSL/SSL imbalance)
- **Kind:** definition
- **What ICT says:** When there's buy-side imbalance (more buyer interest at a level, creating a gap) paired with sell-side efficiency (willingness to sell at higher prices), this creates an inefficiency—a fair value gap. These imbalances are exploited by smart money for directional moves.
- **How to identify:** Look for a gap between candle close and next candle open; one side (buy or sell) is committed; the other (efficiency) shows price unwillingness to commit.
- **How to use:** These imbalances are targets for the opposite direction. Buy-side imbalance with sell-side efficiency = price likely to reverse down into the imbalance.
- **Example in this video:** 15m NQ chart had identified buy-side imbalance and sell-side efficiency pair, shaded as an inefficiency. Michael expected price to trade down and into it, and it delivered precisely.
- **Timestamps:** [0:00:00](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=0) (buy-side imbalance, sell-side efficiency defined) · [0:12:07](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=727) (sell-side liquidity pool: result of BSL/SSL imbalance).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Efficiency is not rejection; it's unwillingness to commit at higher/lower prices, creating a direction bias.

### Low-Hanging Fruit Objective vs. Best-Case Scenario (aliases: LHF, aggressive target, conservative target)
- **Kind:** risk management + position management rule
- **What ICT says:** When you identify a trading setup, define two targets: LHF objective is the reasonable, highly-probable target (where most of the move should complete). Best-case scenario is the aggressive target (lower for shorts, higher for longs) that only happens if momentum continues. Rule: take 80% of your position (bulk) off at LHF. Hold 1–2 contracts as runners to see if you can reach the best case. If LHF happens and best case doesn't, you're still profitable; if best case hits, the runners are extra profit.
- **How to identify:** Calculate LHF as the first major inefficiency or liquidity pool below (for shorts) or above (for longs). Best case = the deepest inefficiency or furthest liquidity target.
- **How to use:** Size your position knowing you'll sell bulk at LHF. Enter, ride to LHF, take profits, hold runners. Don't be greedy; if LHF doesn't hit, you exit; if LHF hits and best case fails, you're satisfied.
- **Example in this video:** Michael identified LHF objective as the minor sell-side liquidity pool. Best case was to continue into the major sell-side efficiency/inefficiency below. He took bulk at LHF and ran rest for best case.
- **Timestamps:** [0:00:49](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=49) (LHF objective vs. best case) · [0:01:37](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=97) (take bulk at LHF, runners for best case).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Psychology: unless you have bulk off at LHF, the best-case target will torture you; take profits.

### Draw on That Low / Draw to Liquidity (aliases: draw, target, confluence draw)
- **Kind:** rule
- **What ICT says:** When price is moving toward a lower timeframe structure (like a 1m inefficiency) that is nested within a higher timeframe (15m) draw, smart money is "drawing" price toward that liquidity or inefficiency. The institutional algorithm pulls price toward these nested draws. If price "wilts" (weakens) into a draw, it's confirming continuation; if it bounces before reaching the draw, the move is questioned.
- **How to identify:** Identify inefficiencies on both 15m and 1m; note when a lower-timeframe inefficiency sits within a higher-timeframe target. That's a draw.
- **How to use:** Use draws as confirmation. If price is moving toward a draw and still showing strength (wicking around efficiently, bodies committed), it's likely to reach it. If weakening before reaching, question the move.
- **Example in this video:** Michael identified a 15m draw (sell-side inefficiency) and a nested 1m inefficiency within it. Price was drawing toward both; the move was so clean it "wilted" exactly into the target inefficiency.
- **Timestamps:** [0:00:49](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=49) (draw = target below) · [0:07:07](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=427) (any movement lower should find momentum build up to get down into that inefficiency) · [0:12:07](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=727) (draw on that low).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Draws aren't random; they're institutional algos targeting nested inefficiencies. Precision comes from nesting timeframes.

### Dealing Range (aliases: range, trading range, overall dealing range)
- **Kind:** definition
- **What ICT says:** A dealing range is a consolidated area or high-to-low range within which price is consolidating. Smaller portions can be identified within larger dealing ranges. These define the boundaries for bounces and reversals.
- **How to identify:** Look at 15m or daily chart; identify a zone where price has been consolidating (high and low clearly defined).
- **How to use:** Use dealing range boundaries as reversal zones; if price is at the low of a range, expect a bounce or continuation lower depending on bias; at the high, expect reversal or continuation higher.
- **Example in this video:** Michael identified a "smaller portion of that overall dealing range identified yesterday," zooming into a subset of the larger 15m range for 1m intraday trading.
- **Timestamps:** [0:02:24](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=144) (smaller portion of that overall dealing range).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Dealing ranges nest; a large daily range contains multiple 15m ranges, each containing multiple 1m ranges.

### First Presented Fair Value Gap (aliases: FPFVG, first presented inefficiency)
- **Kind:** definition + rule
- **What ICT says:** The first presented FVG is the gap that appears when price first breaks into a new area or direction. On subsequent bounces, this same gap may act as a target or rejection level. If it was an inversion FVG (bodies on one side, wicks on other), on the next move it might reverse its role and become a bullish target.
- **How to identify:** Mark the first inefficiency that forms after a break of structure; note its high and low.
- **How to use:** On retracements or reversals, the first presented FVG from an earlier move can act as a bounce or stopping point. Watch to see if it acts as an inversion (bodies respect one side).
- **Example in this video:** Michael identified a first presented FVG and noted it could be used as a target in the afternoon session if price reverses higher. He color-coded it "neutral" (gray) to avoid directional bias.
- **Timestamps:** [0:07:07](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=427) (first presented FVG as target) · [0:11:19](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=679) (first presented fair value gap to watch in PM session).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** FVGs can change roles; tracking the "first presented" one helps you identify direction changes on re-entries.

### Event Horizon (aliases: midpoint, 50% level, halfway point)
- **Kind:** measurement tool
- **What ICT says:** Event Horizon is the midpoint between two price levels (e.g., high and low of an inefficiency, or two historical swing extremes). Price often pauses or reverses at this midpoint. It's a powerful confluence when combined with other factors.
- **How to identify:** Find two price levels (a low and a high, or two equal lows); mark the price midpoint between them.
- **How to use:** Use as a pause/reversal zone. If price is moving toward a low and hits Event Horizon on the way, expect a bounce or consolidation. Also use as a partial profit target.
- **Example in this video:** Michael noted they were "at event horizon between the low of this to the high of that inefficiency." Price was pausing there before continuing lower.
- **Timestamps:** [0:07:56](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=476) (at event horizon, expecting further movement lower after close below).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Event Horizon is a technical level, not an order-flow level; it's useful for timing entries/exits within a larger move.

### Suspension Block (aliases: suspension candle, volume imbalance at high and low)
- **Kind:** definition
- **What ICT says:** A suspension block is a candle with small body and wicks at both high and low (minimal directional commitment). It shows neither bulls nor bears are in control momentarily; the next candle often delivers direction decisively. Michael notes suspension blocks as confirmations of what will happen next (if the next candle is strong down, the suspension preceded a downmove).
- **How to identify:** Look for a candle with nearly equal upper and lower wicks, small or small body.
- **How to use:** Suspension blocks are setup confirmations, not entry signals. Wait for the next candle after suspension for direction; that candle's strength or weakness confirms the next leg.
- **Example in this video:** Michael noted "that's a suspension block" when price showed balanced wicks at both ends. The next candle then broke lower decisively, confirming the downmove.
- **Timestamps:** [0:05:19](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=319) (suspension block noted).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Suspension blocks are pause signals; they're not entries themselves; they're confirmations of what comes next.

### Volume Imbalance (aliases: imbalance, micro-inefficiency, gap between open and close)
- **Kind:** definition
- **What ICT says:** A volume imbalance is when a candle's open is significantly different from its close (more than a tick or two), creating a gap or space. This is part of an inefficiency. If a candle has a 6 and a half close and a 6 and three-quarters open, that's a volume imbalance; use the wick to the next candle's open instead of the close.
- **How to identify:** Compare a candle's open and close; if more than 1–2 ticks apart, it's a volume imbalance.
- **How to use:** For gap construction, use wick-to-next-open rather than close-to-next-open when a volume imbalance is present. It refines your FVG definitions.
- **Example in this video:** Michael checked the close (6.5) vs. the open (6.75) of a candle, found it was a volume imbalance (0.25 difference), so he used the wick to the next candle's open for FVG construction.
- **Timestamps:** [0:11:19](https://youtu.be/N3qz13Hl-gg?t=679) (close 6.5, open 6.75; "that is not a volume imbalance" — it's too large, so use wick).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Volume imbalances appear as gaps visually; they're key to precise FVG definition.

## Rules, checklists & numbers stated
- Low-hanging fruit objective: the reasonable, probable target for bulk of position.
- Best-case scenario: aggressive, deeper target for runner contracts.
- Take bulk of position (80%) off at LHF; hold 1–2 contracts for best case.
- Inversion FVG: bodies stay on one side; if weakening, expect reversal and use as target on reverse move.
- First presented FVG: mark after breaks; can become target on retracements.
- Draw to liquidity: nested inefficiencies on multiple timeframes pull price toward them (institutional algorithm).
- Dealing ranges: consolidation zones; smaller ranges nest within larger ranges.
- Suspension blocks: precede directional breakouts; not entry signals.
- Volume imbalance: open-close difference >2 ticks is significant; use wick-to-next-open for FVG construction.
- Event Horizon: midpoint between two levels; expect pause or reversal.

## Glossary candidates

`Best-case scenario` — an aggressive, secondary target for runner contracts; less probable but high upside if hit.
`Draw to liquidity` — the institutional algorithm targeting nested inefficiencies across timeframes.
`Dealing range` — a consolidation zone; high-to-low boundaries.
`First presented fair value gap (FPFVG)` — the gap that forms first after a break; can become a target on subsequent reversals.
`Event Horizon` — the midpoint between two price levels; often a pause/reversal zone.
