## Concepts

### Event Horizon
- **Kind:** definition + calculation
- **What ICT says:** The midpoint between two significant price levels, calculated by (Level A + Level B) ÷ 2. Event horizon is a price magnet—a level where smart money often holds price temporarily before continuing higher or lower. It's not a target; it's an observation point. When price reaches event horizon, pause and ask: does it have support/resistance there? Can it continue? If price moves through event horizon decisively with strong bodies, the move is likely to continue to the next target.
- **How to identify:** Identify two key levels (e.g., Thursday's daily high at 26,563 and all-time high at 26,859). Calculate midpoint: (26,563 + 26,859) ÷ 2 = 26,711. Mark it on the chart.
- **How to use:** Don't trade at event horizon; use it as a reference. When price is below it, event horizon acts like support (price wants to reach it). When price is above it, it acts like resistance (price wants to push past it to the target). If price stalls at event horizon, it may be a sign of weakness or consolidation.
- **Example in this video:** ES, April 18, 2026. Event horizon = 26,711 (between Thursday's 26,563 high and ATH 26,859). Price ran up from the morning low, reached 26,711, and continued higher to 26,859. Event horizon was a way station, not a reversal point.
- **Timestamps:** [0:04:40](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=280), [0:10:11](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=611), [0:14:56](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=896), [0:30:48](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1848)
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Event horizon can be calculated from any two levels (daily highs, RTH gaps, key wicks, all-time highs). Use it when you need a midpoint reference. It's different from relative equal highs or balanced price ranges—it's purely mathematical, not based on historical price touching.

### Propulsion Block
- **Kind:** entry model + structure
- **What ICT says:** When two consecutive down-close candles form (e.g., in a bearish move), they can be combined into a single "propulsion block" if certain conditions are met. The block's defining feature: the low body (the lower of the two down closes) must stay above the mean threshold (midpoint) of the higher body. In a bullish reversal (after propulsion), price should not retrace into the mean of the propulsion block. If it does, the setup is compromised.
- **How to identify:** Find two consecutive down-close candles. Mark the high and low of each. Draw a line at the midpoint of the upper candle (the mean threshold). The propulsion block is valid if the lower candle's body doesn't cross below that midpoint.
- **How to use:** After a propulsion block forms, it acts as a **defense**—like a bolo or barrier. On a retracement, price should not trade back into the propulsion block's mean; if it does, you're likely to get stopped out. Use it to place stops just below the mean, not inside the block itself. Alternatively, when price breaks above a propulsion block after a bounce, it's a bullish signal.
- **Example in this video:** ICT shows a propulsion block formed near the morning low; price later bounces off it, confirming its defensive function. The bodies stay above the mean threshold, showing respect for the structure.
- **Timestamps:** [0:15:44](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=944), [0:17:21](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1041)
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** "This might be a little unsettling, but that's OK"—sometimes the wick will touch just below the mean and barely miss the low of the order block. This is a rare but valid exception. The rule is bodies staying above the mean, not wicks.

### Bolo (Defensive PD Array)
- **Kind:** definition + defensive structure
- **What ICT says:** A bolo is a defensive PD array formed by the combination of a **rejection block** (a candle that wicks down and back up, rejecting the lower level) and the **consequent encroachment** of that wick. The zone between these two levels is the "bolo"—a defensive line in the sand. You don't trade inside a bolo; it protects your stop loss. If price touches the bolo on a retrace, you'll get stopped out. The name comes from the weapon: a rope with two weights that entangles the legs of a target. The metaphor is that you're trying to "run away" for higher prices, but if you get tangled in the bolo, you trip and fall.
- **How to identify:** Find a rejection candle (wick down, close up). Mark the low of that wick. Draw consequent encroachment (the midpoint of the wick, or a reference level). The zone between the rejection block opening and the wick's consequent encroachment is the bolo.
- **How to use:** Place your stop just above the bolo. Don't penetrate it. If price touches the bolo, you're being "leg-swept" (stopped out). Use it to place mental stops or actual stops to protect your position. It's purely defensive—you're not trading inside it; you're protecting yourself from being caught there.
- **Example in this video:** After the morning push higher, a rejection candle forms and subsequent retracements show respect for the bolo. ICT acknowledges the bolo and warns: "Don't want to see it touched. Treat it like a land mine."
- **Timestamps:** [0:18:57](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1137), [0:19:44](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1184), [0:20:31](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1231)
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** A bolo is one of several "defensive" PD arrays (alongside balanced price ranges). It's not an entry model; it's a structure you learn to respect and avoid.

### Rejection Block
- **Kind:** candle formation + signal
- **What ICT says:** A candle that wicks down significantly (testing a lower level) but then closes higher (near the open or above), rejecting the lower level. This wick shows an attempt to go lower; the close shows a rejection of that lower level. Rejection blocks often mark the point where smart money stops accepting sellers and starts buying again.
- **How to identify:** Look for a candle with a long lower wick and a close near the open or higher. The contrast between the wick low and the close high is the "rejection" signal.
- **How to use:** When you see a rejection block form, it's a bullish signal—price is rejecting lower levels. Use it as a buy signal or as confirmation that a pullback is done. It's often the candle where you'd start adding to long positions or entering longs.
- **Example in this video:** The early morning candle that wicks down but rejects (closes higher) is marked as a rejection block. This forms the top part of the bolo.

### Consequent Encroachment (Wick Midpoint Reference)
- **Kind:** rule + reference level
- **What ICT says:** When a wick extends beyond a support/resistance level, the midpoint of that wick (from the low wick point to the close) becomes "consequent encroachment." This level is a threshold: bodies should not cross it in the opposite direction (if bullish, bodies shouldn't cross below it). If a wick goes down to a low and back up, the midpoint of that wick is a reference line. Futures/scalpers use this as a target level or a rejection point.
- **How to identify:** After a rejection candle (wick down, close up), mark the low of the wick. Mark the close. Calculate the midpoint. That's consequent encroachment.
- **How to use:** Use it as a threshold for body placement. In a bullish setup, bodies should stay above consequent encroachment on any pullbacks. If bodies drop below it, the setup is weakening. In a bearish setup, the opposite: bodies should stay below it. Use it as a reference for partial exits or as a level where you expect price to pause or reverse.
- **Example in this video:** Multiple references to consequent encroachment of key wicks. Bodies must stay above it to show bullish continuation.

- **How to use:** Accept that stop hunting happens. Place your stops tactically, not predictably. Don't announce them publicly (or if you do, as ICT does, expect them to be run). Consider using stops that are far from obvious levels, or use position sizing + mental discipline instead of hard stops. Alternatively, understand that a stop run might be a final capitulation that precedes your move higher—it's a feature, not a bug, of the market structure.

### Inversion Fair Value Gap (AAA Rating / Premium Grade)
- **Kind:** structure + visual confirmation level
- **What ICT says:** Not all inversion FVGs are created equal. A "premium grade" or "AAA" inversion FVG is one where the bodies cannot even touch consequent encroachment (the midpoint/high of the gap) and the bodies don't lay down on top of it at all. This is the strongest signal—the market is showing absolute rejection of anything below the gap, meaning buyers are in complete control and the move is likely to continue without pause.
- **How to identify:** After an inversion FVG forms and price retraces into it, watch the bodies: (1) If they touch or cross the midpoint = normal inversion FVG, (2) If they can't reach the midpoint and stay in the lower half = AAA grade. The AAA signal is the absence of bodies at the midpoint—that's what makes it premium.
- **How to use:** When you see an AAA-grade inversion FVG, it's a strong continuation signal. Add to longs, don't take partials yet, and expect price to run hard. It's the "real one, baby"—ICT's phrase.
- **Example in this video:** The 9:20 fair value gap (inverted) shows an AAA rating: bodies stay well below consequent encroachment, and even the wicks don't touch it. This is a textbook premium-grade inversion FVG, confirming the bullish continuation.
- **Timestamps:** [0:29:12](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1752), [0:30:01](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1801)
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Most inversion FVGs will have bodies touch the midpoint at some point—that's normal. AAA-grade gaps where bodies can't even reach the midpoint are rare and represent the strongest bullish continuation signal.

### Bullish Order Block (CISD—Change in State of Delivery)
- **Kind:** reversal entry model
- **What ICT says:** When a down-close candle prints at a key reversal point (after a series of down closes), it marks the opening price where smart money is now buying. This opening price is a **bullish order block**—the lowest price where buyers are willing to step in. When price retraces back down to that level later, it often acts as support. ICT states: "I'm not selling a bearish down close when I'm looking for 859"—meaning once the order block is identified, the bias has shifted bullish.
- **How to identify:** After a series of down closes, find the last down-close candle that marks the reversal. The opening price of that candle is the bullish order block. Draw a horizontal line at that level.
- **How to use:** Use the bullish order block as a support level for long positions. If price retraces back to it, you can add to longs or expect a bounce. If price closes above the order block, the reversal is confirmed. If price traded below the order block with a body, the reversal may have failed.
- **Example in this video:** The reversal candle from the morning downmove is marked; its opening price becomes the bullish order block for the day's bullish bias.
- **Timestamps:** [0:24:56](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=896), [0:31:41](https://youtu.be/9nmjg331xGQ?t=1901)

## Glossary candidates

`Event horizon` — The midpoint between two significant price levels. Used as a reference point where price often consolidates before continuing or reversing.

`Propulsion block` — Two consecutive down-close candles where the lower candle's body stays above the midpoint of the upper candle. Acts as a defensive barrier on retracements.

`Bolo` — A defensive PD array formed by a rejection candle and its consequent encroachment. A "line in the sand" where price shouldn't retrace; treat it like a landmine.

`Rejection block` — A candle with a long lower wick (testing a lower level) but a close near the open or higher (rejecting the lower level). Bullish signal.

`Consequent encroachment (wick)` — The midpoint of a wick that extends below a key level. Bodies must respect it (stay above it in bullish moves).

`AAA-grade inversion FVG` — A premium-quality inversion FVG where bodies cannot even reach consequent encroachment. Strongest bullish continuation signal.

`Bullish order block` — The opening price of a down-close candle that marks the reversal point. Later acts as support for long positions.

`Stop hunting` — Manual market maker intervention to run stops at a known level, creating panic selling/buying before reversing sharply. Proof it's manual: sudden, fast, deliberate.

`Shinobi mode` — Invisible, no tweets or chart updates posted. Used after a stop hunt when re-entering, to avoid tipping off market makers.

`Minor buy-side liquidity` — Isolated high-volume buying clusters below current price, not major stops but enough to slow a decline or provide resistance.

`Draw on liquidity` — Price approaching a level where smart money expects buyers or sellers to be positioned. The draw itself is not an entry; order flow at that level is.

`Intraday reversal` — A lower-timeframe (1m, 5m) turn that aligns with a higher-timeframe (daily) structure shift. Early pullback to a key level (RTH gap, consequent encroachment) that then reverses higher/lower per the daily bias.
