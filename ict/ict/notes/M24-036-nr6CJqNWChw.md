## Summary
ICT livestreams the Friday 9:30 NY session (NQ, 2024-09-27) after announcing at the open that he expects a "lackluster" morning, then uses the whole session to define two kinds of trading conditions: **low resistance liquidity runs** (fast, precise, algorithmic delivery to the obvious liquidity or inefficiency) and **high resistance liquidity runs** (price keeps teasing the obvious liquidity, retraces deeply, and loses its precision around PD arrays). The signatures he gives are a small opening-range gap (under 75 handles, under 40 is certain), price spending many candles inside fair value gaps or revisiting them repeatedly, and repeated short-term stop runs. He ties these to a bearish Friday narrative built from the opening range gap (70% fill to its midpoint), the first presented 1-minute FVG, TGIF (20–30% of the weekly range), the 9:50–10:10 macro and relative equal lows as the draw. He also gives the management rules for high-resistance days: a stop of at least 2x the FVG range, small size, heavy partials, or no trade and paper/tape-read instead. He insists the diagnostic is simple and visual: if FVGs are not respected quickly, you are in high resistance conditions. Do not call that "Seek and Destroy".

## Concepts

### High Resistance vs Low Resistance Liquidity Run Conditions  (aliases ICT uses: "two types of trading conditions", "high resistance liquidity run days/sessions", "problem market", "lackluster morning")
- **Kind:** definition / rule (a day-classification framework; the core of the video)
- **What ICT says:**
  - There are two trading conditions. **Low resistance:** price goes straight from your entry to the liquidity or inefficiency. Delivery is fast, feedback is immediate, and the day is quick and low-stress. **High resistance:** price struggles to make the easy, fast, straight run to obvious liquidity even though the liquidity is clearly there (here, the relative equal lows). "Resistance" does not mean retail support/resistance. It means resistance to the easy fast run.
  - A high-resistance market teases: it gets close, retraces, consolidates, drops, comes back, and spikes outside inefficiencies. Typically it reaches the liquidity later, after traders have been stopped out or have given up and walked away.
  - Low-resistance days are AI/algorithm-driven, so delivery is "to the tick" and precise around PD arrays. High-resistance days are, in his view, mostly manually intervened: someone is "stirring the pot" with spikes and stop runs, so the precision disappears. Exception: just before a big red-folder event (FOMC, NFP, CPI, PPI) the market marks time sideways. That is not a time to trade either.
  - Losing precision around his PD arrays is not "the algorithm changed" and not "the concept stopped working". It is the sign of manual intervention.
  - High-resistance conditions are not "Seek & Destroy" and not simply "choppy". They just require more time and more experience.
- **How to identify:** (his bullet list at 1:32:19–1:33:05 plus the repeated "one test")
  1. A lackluster opening-range gap: less than 75 handles invites a slow, lethargic morning; under 40 "definitely" means a lackluster morning. A very large gap (he likes about 75 handles or more) is the precursor to a fluid market.
  2. Watch the first 30 minutes (9:30–10:00) to decide which regime you are in.
  3. **The test:** when a fair value gap/inefficiency forms, does price spend a lot of time inside it (multiple candles laid inside, repeated revisits to the same gap or its CE) instead of touching it and repelling quickly? If yes, you are in high-resistance conditions. He says this is all he ever uses to decide, with no hidden PD array.
  4. Price not delivering to the tick on PD arrays; wild spikes beyond inefficiencies; deep retracements; sloppy back-and-forth.
  5. Repeated short-term high runs (buy stops) followed by a sell-off, again and again, when the bias is bearish.
- **How to use:**
  - Recognize it early, ideally before the open from the signatures, and confirm in the first 30 minutes.
  - High resistance: lowest leverage (a micro, or just 1 contract), a wider stop, big partials, or just paper-trade/demo-trade/tape-read. Do not overtrade.
  - Low resistance: push your edge with maximum (not excessive) leverage and take many trades.
  - Classify every day in the journal: "Did the imbalances spend too much time holding price inside them?" Yes means high resistance; no means low resistance.
- **Example in this video:** NQ, Fri 2024-09-27. The opening-range gap was small (a premium gap), so ICT flagged a lackluster morning at 9:30 before anything happened. Price repeatedly rotated inside the first 1-minute FVG and the 15-second FVGs, stabbed up three or four times to run short-term highs, and only later dropped, wicking through the relative equal lows.

### Opening Range Gap (RTH close to 9:30 open) — 70% midpoint fill, quadrants, "no-body zone"  (aliases: opening range gap, ORG, "premium gap", "exhaustion gap")
- **Kind:** definition / rule
- **What ICT says:**
  - The opening range gap is the space between the prior session's regular-trading-hours close/settlement (the 4:14 PM ET RTH close, caption unclear between 4:14 and 4:15) and the 9:30 AM RTH opening price. Use the RTH chart for this. On the ETH (electronic) chart, which runs 6:00 PM–5:00 PM ET (Friday 5 PM close, reopen Sunday 6 PM), it looks different, so make sure you are looking at the right session.
  - The opening range is 9:30–10:00. Algorithms use it as a preliminary range that establishes the first imbalance and the initial highs and lows. You then use that information to build the narrative for how the daily range will unfold.
  - **70%:** about 70% of the time, half of the opening range gap (its consequent encroachment) is traded to in the first 30 minutes. A gap-up opening means the initial bias in the opening range is bearish (he states only the gap-up case; the gap-down mirror is implied, not stated). Sometimes it takes a little longer than 30 minutes.
  - The 9:30 opening price and the prior close are static, non-dynamic reference points. Price can make a higher high in the first few minutes, but the midpoint remains the magnet.
  - Plot the quadrant levels (25/50/75%) on the gap.
  - **"No-body zone" (bearish case):** price may wick into the upper portion of the gap, but ICT does not want candle bodies laid in the upper quadrant, which would make the market more indecisive. Keep bodies out of the upper part and the bearish idea stays intact. Treat the whole gap like a fair value gap: if bearish, any dig back up should stop in the lower half and leave the upper half open. If bullish, leave the lower half open.
  - He also describes a gap-up that sells off, rallies and then folds as something that "could be viewed as an exhaustion gap", where the gap low can then be treated like an FVG.
- **How to identify:** Compare the 9:30 open to the prior RTH close. Mark the gap and the quadrant levels. Note the gap size in handles (see the size thresholds in the High Resistance concept).
- **How to use:**
  - The built-in 70% target gives a high-probability first partial/objective.
  - Gap size sets the regime: large gap (about 75+ handles) leads to a fluid day, small gap (under 75, and especially under 40) leads to a high-resistance day.
  - In his old currency-futures days (British pound, Japanese yen, Canadian dollar) he sold gap-up opens with a roughly 30-handle stop and watched whether the stop or the midpoint was hit first. He said this is an exercise he puts students through (caption somewhat unclear).
- **Example in this video:** NQ 9/27: gap higher open. The gap is shaded and the quadrants drawn. Price tags the gap low and the CE of the first FVG, then wicks into the upper quadrant but the bodies stop at the upper quadrant line, leaving the top slice open. ICT calls that an "algorithmic signature" (the algorithm "not completely closing it in"), which keeps the sell-side draw valid.
- **Timestamps:**
  - [0:05:25](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=325) defines the opening range gap and the 9:30–10:00 opening range.
  - [0:22:26](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=1346) gives the no-body zone.
  - [1:21:52](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=4912) says the 70% midpoint is the static draw.
  - [1:44:30](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6270) explains why the left-open upper portion matters.
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** The 70% figure is stated as a frequency, not a guarantee. If price had fully closed in and traded outside the upper part of the gap, the bearish idea would be much less likely. This builds on the earlier opening-gap teaching; the new part is using gap size to classify the regime.

### First Presented FVG (1-minute, 9:31 or later) as the reference point  (aliases: first FVG of the day, "first presented fair value gap", 9:31 FVG)
- **Kind:** rule / refinement
- **What ICT says:**
  - On the 1-minute chart, the first FVG that forms at 9:31 or later (through 10:00) is your reference. The FVG that includes the 9:30 candle is not used, because of the volatility and the gap, though he notes it is "still useful" to see. On a 15-second chart a gap that forms inside the 9:30 minute is likewise noted but not used.
  - Everybody should start from the same reference point so he can see how students execute and where they go wrong. After that you can use 15, 30 or 45-second charts.
  - Basic read: the market is bullish when above it and bearish when below it. Allow the first-30-minute volatility to trade above and below it. It matters more when it lines up with a macro window (9:50–10:10).
  - Not "enter on the first FVG": the first FVG alone is not an entry. Wait for the conditions around it (below).
- **How to identify:** First 1-minute FVG after the 9:30 candle. Extend it to the right. A down-close candle that price rallies back into is a potential bearish FVG.
- **How to use:** See the entry model in the next concept. In a bearish read, the stop sits above the whole FVG plus the gap height (see Stops).
- **Example in this video:** The first valid 1-minute FVG formed around 9:34–9:35 (he says "9:35" at one point and "34" at another; caption unclear). It is a bearish FVG on a down-close candle, drawn as a darker box. Price initially traded above it with wicks while bodies stayed inside, then displaced below and later returned to kiss its low.

  - [0:40:11](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=2411) gives bullish above / bearish below.

A down-close candle that price rallies into is treated as a bearish FVG, not an inversion FVG, in this video.

### Entry Model: displacement away from the first FVG, retest, then drop to 15-second FVG  (aliases: "the very first fair value gap on the 15 second chart")
- **Kind:** entry model
- **What ICT says:**
  - Do not short just because price is inside the first FVG. Wait for price to displace below it and leave a candle body away from it. Then watch for price to come back up and touch (trade up into the bottom of) the FVG again; he would expect it to trade lower from there.
  - The most conservative version is to require a body close away from below the low of the 1-minute FVG before doing anything. He wants to see a small imbalance form, with the candle not coming back to it yet, and a "breakaway gap" away from the FVG.
  - After the displacement, switch to the 15-second chart. The very first 15-second FVG that forms and is traded back into becomes the catalyst for the short toward the sell-side.
  - This is building an expectation on one timeframe (1m), then executing on another (15s), based on specific parameters. It is not just "pick the first FVG".
- **How to identify:** (1) bearish context (gap-up open, Friday, relative equal lows below); (2) 1m first FVG; (3) body displacement below it; (4) retest of the FVG low/CE; (5) the first 15s FVG on the move down.
- **How to use:** Short the retest; stop above the first FVG plus its height on high-resistance days; target the CE of the opening range gap (70%), then the 20% and 30% weekly-range levels and the relative equal lows. See Stops and Partials below.
- **Example in this video:** After the first 1m FVG, price displaced below it. ICT said "not yet", wanting the candle to leave that small imbalance open first, then to see displacement. When price came back up, wicked, and kissed the low of the first FVG, "that's your short", and the stop goes above the FVG.
- **Timestamps:**
  - [0:25:38](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=1538) explains waiting for displacement below and a retest.
  - [0:27:16](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=1636) explains the 15s FVG as the trigger.
  - [1:46:56](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6416) shows price kissing the low of the first FVG as the short.
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** He admits that during the live session the market did not give the clean sequence ("it should have dropped here, and it didn't") because it was a high-resistance day.

### Stop and size on high-resistance days (stop at least 2x the FVG range)  (aliases: wide stop, "stop has to factor the FVG range")
- **Kind:** rule
- **What ICT says:**
  - On high-resistance days, the stop must factor the entry FVG's range. If you short at the low of a bearish FVG, take the FVG's height and add it above the top of the FVG as well. The stop is at least about 2x the FVG range from entry ("at least two times this range or whatever your favorite gap is").
  - Reason: price can retrace deeply and wick through before still going your way. A wide stop means it takes a real move to stop you, so you can stay in the trade without panic.
  - Because the stop is wide, this likely means a micro-sized position. If you do not want such a wide stop, do not trade that day.
  - Contrast, low-resistance days: stop one tick above the gap's CE (caption somewhat unclear); he says a couple of ticks above the gap is not enough on a high-resistance day.
  - If you have entries early in the day or pre-session, do not rush your stop loss into the range. Let it sit back a while. This is why his own stop management varies between trades (sometimes moved, sometimes left at cost-covering): it depends on which regime he thinks he is in.
- **How to identify:** Regime classification first (above), then size the stop by the width of the reference FVG.
- **How to use:** Entry at the FVG low (short), stop above FVG high plus FVG height, micro size.
- **Example in this video:** The short at the retest of the first 1m FVG, with the stop drawn above the FVG by roughly another gap-height.
- **Timestamps:** [1:46:56](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6416), [1:47:45](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6465) (the 2x range), [1:29:57](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=5397) (do not rush the stop).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** No stated tick or handle count for the stop, only the 2x multiple. This is not a standard ICT stop rule; it applies only to this regime.

### Partials and position sizing by regime  (aliases: "take a partial every time it takes out a low")
- **Kind:** rule
- **What ICT says:**
  - He teaches students to take partials on every new low taken (if short), because they cannot yet tell high-resistance from low-resistance days. Even if you take only 1 contract off, it rewards your patience and finding the setup, and it reduces risk.
  - Position-size example: his standard size is 10 NQ contracts. On a day you already know is high-resistance, a student guessed 6–8 contracts off at the first low taken; he said 6 (or 6–7) is good and 8 is high. The remaining 4 sit through the drawdown (price comes back near the entry) and the same process repeats at the next low.
  - Low-resistance days: use maximum (not excessive) leverage and trade many times. High-resistance days: no lots of trades at high leverage ("like running in quicksand").
  - If already in a trade and you recognize price spending a lot of time in or revisiting the gap before moving your way: drop to the lowest leverage immediately. If trading minis, cut to 1 contract; if micros, to 1 micro. That is risk management, not fear. He compares it to keeping a "dummy wallet" for a hold-up.
  - Taking partials below a newly formed low also fits the liquidity logic: longs have sell stops below that low, and price then tends to wick on through the relative equal lows.
- **How to identify:** After the first low is taken below your entry, partial. Evaluate the regime by the FVG-respect test.
- **How to use:** As above.
- **Example in this video:** The demonstrated short: partials come when price trades below the prior low; after the second move, price wicks through the relative equal lows (the original framing target).
- **Timestamps:**
  - [1:49:22](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6562) covers partials on every low.
  - [1:50:57](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6657) gives the 10-contract exercise and the 6–8 answer.

### TGIF — Friday retracement of 20–30% of the weekly range  (aliases: "Thank God It's Friday", weekly range retracement)
- **Kind:** rule (day-of-week tendency)
- **What ICT says:**
  - After a week that has gone up (a bullish week), he likes to see Friday retrace 20–30% of the weekly range down. After a bearish week he expects a retrace higher. If it does not happen on Friday, he expects a retracement into the prior week's range on Monday.
  - **Measurement:** weekly range = highest high of the week to lowest low of the week. Drop a fib on that range and show the 20% and 30% levels. He changes the 0.25 level to 0.2 (deleting the "5") and toggles 0.3. The levels are measured down from the highest high of the week.
  - Same idea on Mondays is less likely, since it is a new week and the narrative may be different.
  - Use the 20% level as a first partial objective and the 30% level as the next one.
- **How to identify:** A Friday after a week that has traded up (or down) most days, and a clear weekly high/low. Check the levels on the weekly candle and the 1-minute chart so the prices match.
- **How to use:** Combine with a gap-up open (70% midpoint fill) and the first-FVG short. Targets: CE of the opening gap, then the 20% level, then the 30% level.
- **Example in this video:** NQ, Friday of an up week. The 20% level lined up at about 20,421 on both the weekly and the 1-minute chart (caption unclear on the exact figure). Price reached the 30% level inside an FVG, then rolled over again into an inefficiency before breaking down.

### Narrative (not bias) and stacking factors — time, PD arrays, liquidity  (aliases: narrative, "three factors", macro 9:50–10:10)
- **Kind:** rule / definition
- **What ICT says:**
  - "Narrative is not bias." Bias is bullish or bearish. Narrative also says where price is likely to go and why that is realistic.
  - He wants at least three factors behind a trade or an expectation, and one of the main ones must be time. Setups without time lack institutional sponsorship.
  - **Time:** the first 10 minutes before the top of the hour (9:50–10:00) and 10:00–10:10 form a roughly 20-minute macro window. Algorithms start firing and "spooling" price to run to an inefficiency or to liquidity above an old high / relative equal highs or below an old low / relative equal lows. Spooling is not directional.
  - Questions he asks before the open: Where did we open relative to yesterday's 4:14 PM RTH close? Where is the liquidity? Are there relative equal highs or lows? Is there a large inefficiency above or below? Why should price be interested in going higher or lower?
  - **Direction filter:** if you can justify the trade on both the buy side and the sell side, it is not high probability. Prefer days and times when the evidence leans heavily one way.
  - Other factors stacked: Friday/TGIF, an up week, gap-up open, relative equal lows below, an FVG to sell into.
  - Immediate feedback test: if the idea is right, you should see price quickly doing what you expected. If the things that should keep it from going higher are not showing up, you already have feedback that you are not on side.
  - Monday vs Friday: a retracement idea like this is less likely on a Monday because a new week may have an entirely different narrative.
- **How to identify:** A checklist of time + PD array + liquidity + day/week context.
- **How to use:** Only act when several of these line up; if they do not, sit still.
- **Example in this video:** Narrative for the day: Friday, up week, gap-up (premium) open, relative equal lows below as the draw, inside the macro at 9:57, so look for sells and ignore longs ("I don't care if it went up 100 handles").

### Smooth vs jagged — obvious liquidity and retail perception  (aliases: relative equal lows as "support", "smooth lows", "jagged highs")
- **Kind:** definition / refinement
- **What ICT says:**
  - Retail sees relative equal lows as support and expects buying there; stops sit just below (sell stops, or "short buy stops"). That makes them a draw.
  - Price action is "smooth" where liquidity remains untaken (here the relative equal lows below) and "jagged" where it has already been run (the upside highs). The market tends to deliver to the smooth side.
  - Retail chart patterns (bull flags, pennants, wedges, descending triangles) are not the cause of price movement. They only "work" if price is already heading to a liquidity pool. If it is smooth above a bull flag and the downside is already made jagged, the flag may deliver, but only because price is running for liquidity.
  - You do not need a bookmap, DOM or ladder. You can see everything on the chart if you know the time, the day, the inefficiencies and the equal highs/lows.
- **How to identify:** Look for untouched equal highs/lows; compare which side of the market is "smooth".
- **How to use:** Frame the draw toward the smooth side; use obvious retail patterns as a clue to where stops will be.
- **Example in this video:** Relative equal lows below were smooth, the top had been made jagged. Later, bull-flag-looking consolidations were the setups for stop runs.
- **Timestamps:** [0:09:00](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=540), [0:17:41](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=1061), [0:50:30](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=3030).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** The term "smooth/jagged" is used loosely in this video; he does not give a formal definition.

### Inefficiency-respect test: "stick your toe in the water and run away"  (aliases: accumulation inside FVG, touching a gap multiple times)
- **Kind:** rule
- **What ICT says:**
  - In a clean market, price enters or touches the FVG (or its CE) and then repels away quickly: "one quick high-five and you're out of there".
  - Accumulation is fine above old highs or below old lows. It is not fine inside inefficiencies. If price shows lots of interest in getting back into the same gap, or keeps laying candles in it, expect it to go through the gap.
  - He compares it to trend lines: the more times a trend line is touched, the weaker it really is, not stronger.
  - Ideal-world bearish sequence: touch the CE, drop immediately, run right through the low, retrace briefly and continue. On a high-resistance day it instead goes back and forth many times.
- **How to identify:** Count visits to the same FVG and the number of candles inside it.
- **How to use:** If the FVG you are trading from is not respected on the first or second touch, reduce risk (1 contract), widen the stop, or step aside.
- **Example in this video:** Price hit the CE of the first FVG repeatedly and kept coming back instead of failing there; the 15-second gaps behaved the same way ("this many times into it here, here, here").
- **Timestamps:** [1:34:43](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=5683), [1:40:27](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6027), [1:41:16](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6076).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** This is the practical form of the high-resistance diagnostic, restated at [2:16:24](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=8184).

### Stop runs on high-resistance days: Judas swing and turtle soup sells  (aliases: Judas, turtle soup, "manual intervention", "stirring the pot")
- **Kind:** example only / refinement
- **What ICT says:**
  - On a high-resistance day with a bearish bias, price repeatedly runs short-term highs and equal highs, then sells off. To other traders each run looks like a bull flag or a bullish micro-shift, but it is just a run on the buy stops. Shorts' protective buy stops (and breakout buyers' entries) are tripped, so shorts are stopped out and breakout longs are trapped long.
  - The "algorithmic" version is precise. The "manual" version is characterized by spikes outside inefficiencies and long dwell time at the highs.
  - In his explanation, the dwell time at the highs lets the other side build a position: time & sales shows many contracts trading there, and then price shoots lower. Shorts who held through are stopped or buy back at lower prices, while longs' sell stops below the low give liquidity on the way down. Price then wicks through the relative equal lows.
  - If you are bearish, look for turtle soup sells: small bullish-looking market structure shifts that just take short-term buy stops.
  - Judas swing: the early run into the upper part of the opening range gap (before the drop) is the day's Judas swing even though it did not make a higher high than the opening price.
  - Trailing stops get picked off by these runs: traders who trailed their stops lost the trade and did not get "the ticket ride".
- **How to identify:** A string of failed breakouts above short-term highs followed by sell-offs; relative equal highs forming and being run.
- **How to use:** Allow for them with a wider stop; do not trail stops tightly; use the buy-stop runs themselves as short-entry areas once the run completes.
- **Example in this video:** After the first FVG short, price ran up three or four times (including one clear push above a small inefficiency and a lower high that formed relative equal highs), each time repelling aggressively. The second manual push cleared the buy stops, then price took out the low and went to the target.
- **Timestamps:** [1:46:07](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6367) (Judas), [1:54:08](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6848) (stops being "onboarded"), [2:02:56](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=7376) (the relative-equal-high run), [2:14:03](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=8043) (turtle soup sells).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** Much of this is his interpretation of intent ("they want to build positions on a narrow range"); he acknowledges it sounds like a conspiracy and asks viewers to suspend disbelief for the sake of argument. He says he does not need a bookmap to tell this.

### High Resistance vs Seek & Destroy
- **Kind:** refinement / caveat
- **What ICT says:** Do not label a high-resistance day "Seek & Destroy". Students (he mentions Tanya) mislabel hard days as S&D to excuse them. You will not get two S&D days in the same week ("never ever happen"), but you can get a potential S&D day and then a high-resistance day. His example of a real S&D: a Monday earlier in the week/series where he kept himself out of trades (caption unclear on which video).
- **How to identify:** A hard day with many FVG revisits and deep retracements is high resistance; do not file it as S&D just because it is hard.
- **Timestamps:** [2:14:50](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=8090), [2:21:13](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=8473).
- **Nuance / caveats / changes:** This tightens the usage of "Seek & Destroy" from earlier videos in the series; he does not redefine S&D here.

### The reference points work in markets without gaps (forex)
- **Kind:** refinement
- **What ICT says:** In markets that trade 24 hours without an RTH/ETH gap (e.g. forex), still use the same time reference points: yesterday's 4:14 PM level versus today's 9:30 price. The algorithm refers to those price points by time even if you cannot see a gap on your chart, so you get the same simulation as if there were a gap.

## Rules, checklists & numbers stated
- Opening range = 9:30–10:00 ET. Opening range gap = RTH close 4:14 PM ET (prior day) to the 9:30 open (use the RTH chart).
- About 70% of the time, 50% of the opening range gap (CE) is traded to in the first 30 minutes (9:30–10:00).
- Gap size: ideally more than 40 handles; ICT likes about 75 handles. Under 75 handles invites a lethargic morning; under 40 definitely does.
- First valid 1-minute FVG: 9:31 or later (not the 9:30 candle). Same logic for 15-second charts.
- Macro: 9:50–10:00 and 10:00–10:10 (about 20 minutes); algorithms start spooling then.
- TGIF: after an up week, expect a 20–30% retracement of the week's range (highest high to lowest low) on Friday, or on Monday if it does not happen Friday. Reverse for a down week. Fib levels 0.2 and 0.3.
- Bearish idea: no candle bodies in the upper quadrant of the opening range gap (wicks are fine).
- Bearish FVG: the upper half should remain open when price returns to it. Bullish FVG: the lower half should remain open.
- At least three factors in a trade idea, one of them time.
- High-resistance stop: at least 2x the entry FVG's range (entry at FVG low, stop above FVG high plus FVG height); small size/micro.
- Partials: take one on every new low taken (short). With 10 contracts on NQ on a known high-resistance day, around 6 (up to 7) off at the first low; 8 is high.
- If you recognize high resistance while in a trade, cut to 1 contract (mini) or 1 micro.
- Low-resistance days: maximum (not excessive) leverage and many trades. High-resistance days: lowest leverage or paper/tape-read.
- ETH trades 6:00 PM–5:00 PM ET; Friday closes at 5:00 PM and reopens Sunday 6:00 PM ET.
- You will not see two Seek & Destroy days in the same week.
- Stated standard size: 10 NQ contracts (his).

## Process, risk & psychology
- Learn when not to trade first; "there's always an invitation for you to study". ([0:35:22](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=2122))
- Avoid forcing a trade when the setup is "not yet". Sit still, paper trade, demo trade, or tape-read; no entry means no loss, no regret. ([0:47:19](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=2839))
- Do not overtrade into a hard day just because it is Friday. Losing on Friday and tilting can wipe out the week's profits and make for a painful weekend and a bad Monday. ([0:52:04](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=3124))
- On high-resistance days, trade 1 micro or demo and count how many attempts it takes to get one winner, including commissions and NFA fees. Many "I was right" wins end net negative once costs and drawdown are counted. ([0:58:29](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=3509), [0:59:16](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=3556))
- Do not chase other people's influence, and do not use streamers' P&L as a guide. Honest streamers who say "I don't know what to do right now" show maturity. ([1:03:17](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=3797))
- Stop thinking about "getting back to break even"; journal when you feel the urge. Remind yourself you have a tested model and there is no emergency. ([1:07:19](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=4039), [1:08:11](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=4091))
- Do not trade your equity curve or treat prop resets as free; the reset button trains you to fail consistently. ([1:08:59](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=4139), [1:09:49](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=4189))
- Journal daily as a "board meeting with yourself": the first rule of risk management is recognizing when risk is elevated. Classify each day by whether imbalances hold price inside them. ([1:37:05](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=5825), [2:30:01](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=9001))
- Take partials to reward patience and cut risk; reduce to the lowest size when you spot the regime ("dummy wallet" idea). ([1:49:22](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=6562), [2:09:16](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=7756))
- Beginners should not trade high-resistance days; if you made money on one, do not treat it as proof. Having been burned once gives you something to measure future expectations against. ([1:17:50](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=4670))
- He says the aim is to anticipate (not react to) price: read the conditions in advance, as he did on the stream at the open. ([2:06:06](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=7566))
- Focus on easy days; trading should be enjoyable, and if it is stressful you are probably overleveraged or overtrading. ([2:27:40](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=8860))
- Do not use large leverage (funded-account style) in mornings with a small gap; a big gap is the first sign that larger risk may be justified. ([0:33:43](https://youtu.be/nr6CJqNWChw?t=2023))

## Glossary candidates
`HIGH RESISTANCE LIQUIDITY RUN` — a condition where price resists making the fast, easy run to obvious liquidity; marked by many revisits to FVGs, deep retracements and lost precision.
`LOW RESISTANCE LIQUIDITY RUN` — a clean, fluid condition: price delivers to the tick, fast, with immediate feedback.
`MANUAL INTERVENTION` — ICT's term for non-algorithmic price delivery (stop runs, spikes), shown by loss of precision around PD arrays.
`ALGORITHMIC SIGNATURE` — a precise or telling price behavior attributed to the algorithm (e.g. leaving the upper part of the opening range gap open).
`FIRST PRESENTED FVG` — the first FVG that forms at 9:31 or later on the 1-minute chart (or equivalent on 15s).
`OPENING RANGE GAP` — gap between the prior day's 4:14 PM RTH close and the 9:30 open; 50% (CE) is hit about 70% of the time in the first 30 minutes.
`TGIF` — Friday retracement of 20–30% of the weekly range after a trending week.
`HANDLES` — index points (NQ); used to size the opening gap (40 / 75).
`NO-BODY ZONE` — the upper quadrant of a gap-up opening range gap where he does not want to see candle bodies for a bearish idea (my phrase for what he calls a "no-no zone").
`GAMBLER'S NUMBNESS` — impulsive clicking after a loss to escape the pain; related to tilt.
`TURTLE SOUP SELL` — in a bearish read, a short-term high run that looks like a bullish shift but only takes buy stops before dropping.
`TAPE READ` — watching price live without executing (paper/demo) for practice.
`ONBOARDING STOPS` — ICT's phrase for smart money taking the other side of stops as they are triggered.
